🪪 Weingarten Rights — Your Meeting Shield

IF A MEETING COULD LEAD TO DISCIPLINE, SAY THIS WORD-FOR-WORD:

“If this discussion could in any way lead to my being disciplined or terminated, I request that my union steward or representative be present at this meeting. Until my representative arrives, I choose not to answer any questions.”

📞 Call steward now
  • You must ask. The employer has no duty to tell you about this right.
  • It covers investigatory interviews — questions that could lead to discipline. Not routine work instructions.
  • If they refuse and keep questioning, stay calm, don't walk off the job — answer nothing, take notes, and call the steward right after.
  • Never sign a statement, waiver, or “last chance agreement” before the steward reads it.

NLRB v. J. Weingarten, Inc., 420 U.S. 251 (1975)

FEDERAL Rights on Every Job

Section 7 of the National Labor Relations Act protects “concerted activity” — two or more workers (or one acting for others) raising pay, safety, scheduling, or any working condition. That includes comparing wages in the break room, group texts about conditions, refusing as a group, and organizing.

The employer cannot legally threaten, interrogate, spy on, discipline, or fire you for it. Those are unfair labor practices — file with the NLRB within 6 months. Charges are free, you don't need a lawyer, and the steward can help write one. 29 U.S.C. §157, §158(a); charge deadline §160(b). nlrb.gov · 1-844-762-6572
You have the right to a workplace free of recognized serious hazards, the right to request an OSHA inspection (your name stays confidential if you ask), the right to see the injury log (OSHA 300) and safety data sheets for every chemical you work around, and required PPE at the employer's cost.

You can refuse imminently dangerous work if the danger is real and urgent, you asked the employer to fix it, there's no time for an OSHA inspection, and you stay on site. The Safety page has the word-for-word script.

Punished for raising safety? That's illegal retaliation — but the complaint window is only 30 days. OSH Act §11(c); 29 CFR 1977.12; PPE 29 CFR 1910.132. Fed OSHA 1-800-321-6742 · MN OSHA does most enforcement here
Federal law: 1.5× pay after 40 hours in a week. “Hours worked” includes setup and cleanup you're required to do, mandatory meetings and training, and travel between job sites during the day. Working off the clock is illegal even if you “volunteer” — the employer must pay for work it knows about. Retaliation for a wage complaint is itself a violation.

Keep your own hours record (photos of timecards, a notes app). Claims reach back 2 years — 3 if the violation was willful. Fair Labor Standards Act, 29 U.S.C. §207, §215(a)(3), §255
Up to 12 weeks of job-protected leave per year for your own serious health condition, a new child, or caring for a close family member — if you've worked 12 months and 1,250 hours, and the employer has 50+ employees within 75 miles. Unpaid under federal law, but Minnesota Paid Leave now pays benefits for much of the same time — see the Benefits page. Health insurance continues during FMLA leave. 29 U.S.C. §2601 et seq.
On federal construction contracts over $2,000, Davis-Bacon requires the posted prevailing wage and fringe for your classification — check the wage determination posted at the site office. Minnesota has its own prevailing wage law for state-funded projects. If your rate looks light on a public job, that's a steward conversation and potentially a Department of Labor complaint — contractors must keep certified payroll. Davis-Bacon Act, 40 U.S.C. §3141; Minn. Stat. 177.41–.44
The union owes every member the duty of fair representation — grievances handled honestly and without favoritism. You're entitled to a copy of the contract, to speak and vote at membership meetings, to run for office, and to see the union's annual financial reports (LM forms — public at dol.gov/olms). Vaca v. Sipes, 386 U.S. 171 (1967); LMRDA, 29 U.S.C. §401 et seq.
Title VII bans discrimination by race, color, religion, sex, and national origin; the ADEA covers age 40+; the ADA requires reasonable accommodation for disabilities (including many injuries) unless it's an undue hardship — an interactive conversation the employer must take seriously. USERRA protects National Guard and Reserve members' jobs through deployments and drills. Harassment that's severe or pervasive is discrimination too.

EEOC deadline: 300 days in Minnesota. The state route (MDHR) allows 1 year and Minnesota's Human Rights Act covers more categories. 42 U.S.C. §2000e; 42 U.S.C. §12101; 38 U.S.C. §4301; Minn. Stat. ch. 363A

MINNESOTA Stronger Than Federal

Minnesota passed a wave of worker laws in 2023–2024. Most members don't know half of these exist.

Since 2019, intentional wage theft in Minnesota is a criminal offense — up to felony level. You're entitled to a written notice of your pay rate at hire and before any change, plus an itemized earnings statement every payday. Shorted hours, skimmed OT, unpaid show-up time, and misapplied rates all count.

Document, then call the steward and MN DLI Labor Standards (651-284-5075). Complaints can be anonymous. Minn. Stat. 181.032; 609.52 subd. 2(a)(19)
Statewide since Jan 1, 2024: you earn 1 hour of paid sick time per 30 hours worked (up to 48 hrs/year accrued). Use it for your own illness or appointments, a sick family member, school/daycare closures, or safety needs related to domestic abuse. The employer cannot demand a doctor's note for short absences or retaliate for using it. Your balance must appear on your earnings statement. Minn. Stat. 181.9445–.9448
Since August 2023, a Minnesota employer cannot discipline you for refusing to attend employer-sponsored meetings whose main purpose is pushing the employer's religious or political views — and that explicitly includes the employer's opinions about unions. If an anti-union meeting gets called, you can stand up and walk out, and punishing you for it is illegal. Minn. Stat. 181.531
Minnesota law separately guarantees your right to disclose your own wages to anyone — on top of the federal NLRA protection. “Pay secrecy” policies and gag rules are unenforceable, and retaliation is illegal. Comparing checks is how wage theft and unequal pay get caught. Minn. Stat. 181.172
Make a written request and the employer must let you review your personnel record (once per 6 months while employed) and give you a free copy after review. Disagree with something in it? You can submit a written rebuttal that must be kept with the file. Smart move before and after any discipline. Minn. Stat. 181.960–.966
Minnesota is one of the strictest states: testing requires a written policy you've seen, a confirmatory lab test before any action, and your right to request a retest of the same sample. For employees, a first confirmed positive cannot mean automatic firing — the employer must first offer a chance at counseling or treatment. Random testing of non-safety-sensitive employees is restricted.

If a test comes back positive, say nothing, sign nothing, call the steward. Minn. Stat. 181.950–.957
Workers' comp is no-fault: it doesn't matter if the injury was partly your mistake. Report it to a supervisor the same day (creates the official record), get medical care, and in Minnesota you can generally treat with your own doctor. It covers medical bills, wage-loss benefits, and permanent injury compensation. Sign no medical releases or settlement papers until the union or a comp attorney reviews them — and firing or threatening you for filing is illegal and carries damages.

See the Safety page for the step-by-step injury checklist. Minn. Stat. ch. 176; retaliation 176.82; notice 176.141
Report a violation of law — safety, wage, fraud, anything — in good faith to the employer or a government body, or refuse an order you reasonably believe is illegal, and Minnesota's Whistleblower Act protects you from retaliation. Keep your report in writing where possible. Minn. Stat. 181.932
Any non-compete agreement signed on or after July 1, 2023 is void and unenforceable for employees and independent contractors in Minnesota, regardless of pay level. An employer waving one at you on the way out is bluffing (confidentiality and non-solicit clauses are a different conversation — ask before signing anything). Minn. Stat. 181.988
Breaks: restroom break each 4 consecutive hours; sufficient meal time when working 8+ hours. Fired? Final wages are due within 24 hours of your written demand. Quit? Next regular payday (within 20 days). Late final pay can stack a penalty of a day's wages per day late, up to 15 days. Minn. Stat. 177.253–.254; 181.13–.14
Minnesota's 2024 misclassification law hits construction hard: a worker on a building site is an employee unless the contractor proves a strict 14-factor test. Misclassified workers lose comp coverage, unemployment, OT, and benefits — and contractors now face major penalties, with general contractors on the hook for subs' violations. If someone on your site is paid “1099” for crew work, the union wants to know. Minn. Stat. 181.723 (as amended 2024)
Minnesota requires reasonable accommodations for pregnancy (water, seating, lifting limits, more frequent breaks — no doctor's note needed for the basics), paid lactation breaks with a private space that isn't a bathroom, and protects against retaliation. Minnesota Paid Leave covers bonding time for new parents — see Benefits. Minn. Stat. 181.939

🧾 Called Into the Office? Run This Checklist

  1. Ask what it's about. If it could lead to discipline → say the Weingarten line.
  2. Get the steward there before answering anything: 612-428-8004.
  3. Sign nothing — not a statement, not a write-up acknowledgment marked “I agree,” not a last-chance agreement, not a medical release.
  4. Stay on the job unless it's unsafe — “work now, grieve later” protects your record. (Unsafe is different: see refusing unsafe work.)
  5. Write it down the same day — who, what, when, witnesses — in the private incident log on this site.
  6. Request your personnel file in writing — it's your right (Minn. Stat. 181.961) and it locks the record.
