⚠️ Right to Refuse Unsafe Work

You can refuse work that poses imminent danger to your life or health. Tell the supervisor why the task is unsafe and request a steward immediately.

Sheet metal example: After a fire, chemical spill, failed ventilation, or major equipment damage, ask for hazard assessment before restart. Do not work where there is immediate serious risk.

For non-immediate hazards: document the condition, report it in writing to management, and contact your steward so timelines are protected.

Say this word-for-word:

"I believe this work poses an imminent danger to my safety. I'm requesting a hazard assessment and my union steward before I continue."

🪜 Fall Protection — 6 Feet Is The Line

OSHA requires fall protection at 6 ft (construction) or 4 ft (general industry). Scaffolds: 10 ft. Steel erection: 15 ft.

  • Anchor point: rated 5,000 lb per worker (or 2× fall force if engineered).
  • Harness: inspect webbing, D-rings, buckles before each use. Replace after any fall.
  • Lanyard: shock-absorbing, max free fall 6 ft. Connect high — at/above the D-ring.
  • Ladders: 4-to-1 angle, 3 ft extension above landing, maintain 3 points of contact.
  • Rescue plan: required before work starts. Suspension trauma kills in 15 min.

🔒 Lockout / Tagout (LOTO) — No Shortcuts

The 6-step LOTO procedure (OSHA 1910.147):

  1. Prepare — know all energy sources (electrical, hydraulic, pneumatic, thermal, stored).
  2. Shut down — use normal stop procedure.
  3. Isolate — disconnect switches, close valves, block lines.
  4. Lockout/Tagout — your lock, your key. Tag with name, date, reason.
  5. Release stored energy — bleed lines, block/chain falling parts, discharge capacitors.
  6. Verify zero energy — try to start it. Test with meter. Then work.

⚠️ Never remove another worker's lock.

If a lock was left on, contact your supervisor AND steward. There's a formal removal procedure — it is not your call.

😷 Silica, Metal Fumes & Welding — The Air You Breathe

Sheet metal work exposes you to hexavalent chromium, manganese, zinc oxide, nickel, and silica. These cause lung disease, cancer, and neurological damage — years later.

  • Local exhaust ventilation (LEV) required within 12 in of the fume source.
  • Stainless / galvanized / coated steel — respirator required (APF 10+ for Cr(VI)).
  • OSHA PEL — Cr(VI): 5 μg/m³ (8-hr TWA). Action level: 2.5 μg/m³.
  • OSHA PEL — Respirable silica: 50 μg/m³ (8-hr TWA).
  • Medical surveillance required if exposed above action level ≥ 30 days/yr.
  • Fit test required annually and when respirator/facial hair/weight changes.

If LEV is broken or missing, stop work and call your steward. Exposure records are your records — you can request them.

⚠️ Report a Near-Miss — Save The Next Guy

A near-miss is a warning. Reporting it creates a paper trail and forces the company to fix it before someone gets hurt. This stays in your phone until you email it.

🌡️ Heat Illness Prevention — Know Your Rights

⚠️ Current Status (Feb 2026)

Federal OSHA proposed a heat rule, but it is not final yet. You are still protected right now:

  • Federal OSHA can cite dangerous heat conditions under the General Duty Clause and heat-focused enforcement.
  • Minnesota Rule 5205.0110 sets indoor heat exposure limits based on work intensity (WBGT), not just air temperature.

Minnesota indoor heat rule (WBGT limits)

What is WBGT? WBGT stands for Wet Bulb Globe Temperature. It is a heat stress index measured with a special gauge that accounts for air temperature, humidity, wind, and radiant heat (e.g., from machinery or sun). Unlike a regular thermometer, WBGT better reflects how hot it feels to your body when working. MN Rule 5205.0110 uses WBGT — not plain air temperature — to set safe exposure limits.

For acclimatized, fully clothed workers, MN Rule 5205.0110 sets two-hour average limits:

  • Heavy work: 77°F WBGT
  • Moderate work: 80°F WBGT
  • Light work: 86°F WBGT

These are regulatory exposure limits. Employers still need practical controls (water, cooling, training, pacing) before workers reach dangerous conditions.

What employers should provide

  • Cool drinking water and frequent hydration opportunities
  • Cooling methods (fans, ventilation, shade/rest areas)
  • Acclimatization for new or returning workers
  • Training on heat illness signs and emergency response
  • Work-rest pacing and monitoring when heat risk is elevated

🚨 Warning Signs

Heat Exhaustion

Heavy sweating, dizziness, nausea, headache. Action: Move to shade, cool water, rest.

Heat Stroke (EMERGENCY)

No sweating, hot/red skin, confusion, seizures. Action: CALL 911, cool with water/ice.

✊ What You Can Do Right Now

  • Ask for water, cooling, and recovery breaks — your employer must provide a safe workplace
  • Report unsafe heat: MN OSHA 651-284-5050 or Federal OSHA 800-321-6742
  • You cannot be fired for reporting safety concerns — retaliation is illegal
  • New or returning workers are at higher risk; ask for gradual ramp-up
  • Contact your steward if management ignores heat hazards
